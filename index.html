    Monte Carlo: 100,000 players at one win rate

    Each simulated player wins each match with probability p and stops at their first loss. Theory says the number who win exactly r matches is n · pr(1 − p), a geometric distribution. The bars are the simulation and the dots are the formula.

    The math

    Ties replay, so every match ends in a win or a loss. If you win each match with probability p, and the matches are independent, the chance of winning all 33 is

    P(champion) = p33

    At p = ½ that is 2−33 ≈ 1.16×10−10, or 1 in 8.59 billion. That is slightly worse than 1 in 8.32 billion, because 233 = 8,589,934,592 is the bracket size once you pad the field with byes.

    Small edges per match compound into huge differences at the end, because log P = 33 · log p. Going from 50% to 60% multiplies your odds by 1.233 ≈ 412.

    What the studies found

    Wang, Xu & Zhou (2014), Scientific Reports

    360 students at Zhejiang University played 300 rounds each, in 60 groups of six, for real cash. Overall they threw rock, paper and scissors at 36%, 33% and 32%, close to the ⅓-⅓-⅓ Nash equilibrium. Their throws from one round to the next were not independent, though. Winners were more likely to repeat their throw than to switch in either direction. For most payoff settings, losers tended to shift "clockwise" (R→S, S→P, P→R), meaning they switched to the throw that loses to the one they just played.

    The authors call this a win-lose-tie conditional response model and show it predicts the persistent cycling they saw across whole groups.

    Brockbank & Vul (2021), Games

    Pairs of people played up to 300 rounds against the same opponent. This game's opponents are built from that data set: players, throws. For each player with at least 100 rounds, we measured how they responded to a win, a tie and a loss, then smoothed each row toward the population average with 6 pseudo-observations.

    The population averages are below. The strongest signal is after a loss: people stayed only of the time, well below the ⅓ you'd expect from random play. Win-stay is much weaker here than in the 2014 study. Most of the edge comes from individual players, who differ a lot.
